\section{Discussion}

The estimator is not a pure cumulant-propagation or covariance-propagation method \citep{wright2024analytic, wu2026estimating}. Instead, it combines analytical approximations with antithetic Sobol sampling. Its primary mechanistic contribution is using structural information about the MLP to sample more strategically: the diagonal Gaussian pass first predicts which neurons are dead, kink, and on; the pilot samples refine uncertain classifications; and the resulting activation structure determines both which neurons are sampled and how the sampled matrix products are evaluated. We hope our methods can serve as a sampling baseline for more sophisticated white-box estimators that exploit the same mechanistic structure.

A useful stress test is to ask what the estimator's premises depend on. We run two ablation studies, varying the nonlinearity and the architecture independently, to see whether the estimator's assumptions survive. Each ablation is run on a matched random MLP with width 256, depth 32, and He-scaled Gaussian weights.

The first ablation varies the \emph{nonlinearity}. \Cref{fig:activation-function-regimes,fig:activation-function-design-matrices,fig:activation-function-covariance} repeat the diagnostic with ReLU, GELU \citep{hendrycks2016gelu}, and SiLU \citep{elfwing2018silu}. The result is not activation-agnostic. ReLU creates literal always-off and always-on regions: 150 of the 256 neurons at the scored layer have a pre-activation whose sign is decided before the input is seen, and $49.6\%$ of the scored matrix is exactly zero, which is what justifies exact dead pruning and linear folds. GELU and SiLU produce neither --- no decided-sign neurons and no exact zeros at all --- so there is nothing to prune or fold regardless of what else they do. What changes instead is the scale: SiLU attenuates by ${\times}0.72$ per layer, so its typical activation falls to $4.6\times10^{-5}$ by depth 32 while its firing-rate structure stays close to ReLU's. This distinction matters methodologically as well as mechanistically. Any diagnostic that classifies neurons by an absolute magnitude cutoff will report an entire SiLU layer as dead once the decaying scale passes that cutoff, which is a property of the threshold and not of the network; the views in \cref{fig:activation-function-regimes} avoid it by plotting magnitude and firing rate as separate, threshold-free quantities. The broader lesson is still mechanistic: inspect the activation distribution and sample matrix first, then choose the computation that matches that network's behavior.

The second ablation varies the \emph{architecture} instead, and is read off the identical three plots. \Cref{fig:architecture-regimes,fig:architecture-design-matrices,fig:architecture-covariance}, summarized in \cref{tab:architecture-ablation}, compare six wirings on the same weight tensors --- plain $\relu(Wx)$, a residual block $x + \relu(Wx)$, batch, layer, and RMS normalization applied to the pre-activation, and a pre-norm residual block $x + \relu(W\,\mathrm{LN}(x))$ --- so any difference is attributable to the wiring alone. BatchNorm is used in its inference-time form, a fixed per-neuron affine calibrated from population statistics. The estimator's premises do not survive uniformly. In the plain stack, the scored layer splits $73/106/77$ into dead, kink, and always-on, and the cold prefix that the packed-prefix rule can skip exactly averages $35.3\%$ of the width. Under BatchNorm and under the pre-norm residual block, every one of the 256 neurons at the scored layer is kink: standardizing each neuron to zero mean and unit variance leaves no pre-activation with a persistent sign, and normalizing the branch input keeps the residual stream well enough conditioned to do the same. The cold prefix collapses to about $2\%$ of the width in both cases. Layer normalization sits in between, weakening the dead and always-on populations to $60/66$ without erasing them.

RMS normalization is a special case worth stating exactly, because it changes nothing at all. RMSNorm rescales each sample by a positive scalar, and $\relu$ is positively homogeneous, so by induction over depth the sign pattern of every pre-activation is identical to the plain network's. Empirically the dead and always-on masks are bit-identical and the largest firing-rate discrepancy is $5\times10^{-5}$, exactly one sample in $20{,}000$ --- a float32 tie at zero. LayerNorm subtracts a mean and is therefore not positively homogeneous, which is precisely why it does move the regimes. Any normalization of this family is free for the estimator: it can be folded into the weights and ignored.

The residual row is the one that needs a caveat rather than a headline. The unnormalized block $x + \relu(Wx)$ appears to be the best case of all --- rank $1.0$, only five kink neurons at the scored layer, and $100\%$ of its zeros inside the cold prefix --- but its residual stream reaches an RMS of $1.5\times10^{7}$ by depth 32, so the collapse is a symptom of unchecked variance growth rather than of the skip connection. Rescaling the branch, as SkipInit, Fixup, and the stable-ResNet $1/\sqrt{L}$ scaling do, separates the two: at $1/\sqrt{L}$ the stack returns to $83/98/75$ with effective rank $9.5$, essentially the plain network, and at branch scale $0.1$ it goes to $22/217/17$ with rank $68.9$. Residual \emph{topology} does not help the estimator; unnormalized residual \emph{growth} does, and no trained network is left in that state.

Two things generalize from this. First, the amount of zero mass is not what varies. Every architecture here leaves $47$--$50\%$ of the scored activation matrix at exactly zero; what varies by a factor of thirty is how much of that zero mass lands in a contiguous cold prefix that can be skipped without gathering ($91\%$ of the zeros for the plain stack, $87\%$ under LayerNorm, but $3.0\%$ under BatchNorm and $2.2\%$ under the pre-norm residual). Sparsity is architecture-invariant; its \emph{exploitability} is not, and the packed-prefix machinery is what converts one into the other. Second, effective rank organizes most but not all of the picture, and putting the two ablations on one pair of axes (\cref{fig:rank-collapse}) shows how unequal they are. Across the nonlinearities the scored-layer effective rank spans only $1.4$ to $4.1$; across the architectures it spans $1.0$ for the residual stack, $2.2$ plain, $9.4$ under BatchNorm, and $94.8$ under the pre-norm residual --- roughly two orders of magnitude --- and the exploitable fraction tracks it across those four. RMSNorm is the counterexample that keeps this from being a law: it lifts effective rank from $2.2$ to $10.9$ while leaving every regime label unchanged, because rank is a statement about magnitudes and the classification is a statement about signs. Between the two ablations, then, it is the architecture and not the activation function that decides whether there is anything for the estimator to exploit.

Placing both ablations on one scale (\cref{tab:polarization,tab:degeneracy-axes}) sharpens what "degeneracy" is doing here, because it turns out to name three separable things that no variant optimizes together. \Cref{tab:polarization} takes the estimator's own view: what fraction of neurons is polarized enough to decide analytically. ReLU leaves only $10\%$ of neurons in the ambiguous middle band, and the residual stack none at all, whereas GELU and SiLU leave roughly two thirds there and BatchNorm and the pre-norm residual leave all of them. \Cref{tab:degeneracy-axes} then splits degeneracy into hard structure, scale stability, and rank retention, and the three do not move together. SiLU retains the most rank of the activations ($4.10$) but at a scale that decays by $\times 0.72$ per layer, so by depth 32 its typical activation is $4.6\times10^{-5}$; GELU holds a workable scale but collapses to effective rank $1.43$, the lowest of any variant we ran; the unnormalized residual stack does the reverse, growing at $\times 1.67$ per layer into rank $1.00$. What matters for the estimator is none of these directly --- it is the first two columns, and only the ReLU family has them at all. GELU and SiLU produce exactly zero exact zeros and zero decided-sign neurons, so there is nothing to prune or fold no matter how the rank and scale behave.

The unifying reading is that what the estimator exploits is degeneracy of one specific kind --- neurons whose sign is decided before the input is seen, and a representation that has collapsed onto few enough directions to make that common. Normalization is engineered specifically to prevent degeneracy, which is why it is also what disables the method. The practical consequence is that the compute savings reported here should be expected to be largest on exactly the networks that are worst conditioned, and to shrink toward the plain-sampling baseline on well-normalized ones. That is a real limitation of scope, but it is a legible one: the diagonal Gaussian pass measures the dead and always-on populations directly and cheaply, so a deployed estimator can detect that it is in the unfavorable regime before committing to a plan, and fall back rather than fail.

For the purposes of the challenge, the result shows that white-box structure can substantially improve the compute-accuracy tradeoff over plain sampling. For the broader mechanistic-estimation agenda, the lesson is narrower but encouraging: even a simple Gaussian regime classifier can turn a random MLP into a smaller estimation problem whose remaining uncertainty is localized and measurable.
