\section{Results, Limitations, and Future Work}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{The Scored Error Concentrates in a Few Large Always-On Neurons}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/final_layer_error_by_class.png}
    \caption{Final-layer error by neuron class (50 public mini networks). Always-on neurons carry 74.4\% of the total squared error, kink neurons 24.8\%, dead neurons 0.8\%.}
    \label{fig:error-by-class}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{figures/final_layer_error_concentration.png}
    \caption{Cumulative final-layer squared error by output-magnitude rank (same 50 networks): the top 10\% of neurons carry 38\% of the squared error, the top 20\% carry 61\%.}
    \label{fig:error-concentration}
\end{figure}

All measurements in this section use the submitted estimator (submission \submissionid, $N = 84{,}992$ Sobol samples) on 50 of the 100 public mini networks (even ids 0--98). For distribution-level measurements we take four of these networks (ids 0, 32, 66, 98) and draw 16{,}384 antithetic samples each.\footnote{All statistics are produced by \texttt{estimator\_error\_attribution\_319341.ipynb} in the repository; the figures are rendered by \texttt{writeup/figures.ipynb} from the same cached predictions.} Since only final-layer error is scored, we restrict attention to the final layer, where the median MSE is $2.3\times10^{-7}$ (mean $2.9\times10^{-7}$).

When we analyze the final-layer squared error by neuron class (\Cref{fig:error-by-class}), always-on neurons turn out to carry most of it---74.4\% of the total, against 24.8\% for kink neurons and just 0.8\% for dead ones. This is not because on neurons are more numerous but because they are bigger: their outputs average $\approx 1.5$ in magnitude versus $\approx 0.17$ for kink neurons, so even modest relative errors on the on block dominate everything else. The concentration continues within the on block itself (\Cref{fig:error-concentration}), where the top 10\% of neurons by output magnitude account for 38\% of the squared error and the top 20\% for 61\%.

\subsection{The On-Neuron Error Is Sampling Variance, Not Bias}

Is the on-block error bias or just noise? Pooled over the 50 networks, the mean signed error is $-2.0\times10^{-5}$---roughly forty times smaller than the error spread of $8.6\times10^{-4}$, so its squared contribution is under $0.1\%$ of the MSE---and it has no meaningful direction. There is a slight lean toward under-estimation (51.4\% of on neurons land below the truth, as do 28 of the 50 networks), but this is a median effect rather than a bias: frequent small undershoots are balanced by rarer, larger overshoots, so more than half of realizations land low. Because all 256 outputs share the same sample points, each network additionally picks up a common shift, which is why whole networks (not just neurons) lean one way. Further analysis also indicates that the size of the error matches what sampling variance predicts.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work: Heavy Tails and Jointly-Crossing Kinks}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \begin{minipage}{0.49\linewidth}
        \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/on_neuron_kurtosis_depth.png}
    \end{minipage}\hfill
    \begin{minipage}{0.49\linewidth}
        \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/on_neuron_skewness_depth.png}
    \end{minipage}
    \caption{Excess kurtosis (left) and skewness (right) of on-neuron post-ReLU activations versus depth (four probe networks; thin lines are individual networks, bold their mean). Both grow with depth, consistent with sub-Weibull tails.}
    \label{fig:heavy-tails}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[t]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.62\linewidth]{figures/kink_preactivation_correlation.png}
    \caption{Mean absolute pairwise correlation of kink-neuron pre-activations versus depth (same four probe networks), growing from $\approx 0.13$ in early layers to $\approx 0.21$ in late layers.}
    \label{fig:kink-correlation}
\end{figure}

Prior work shows that on-neuron activations become increasingly heavy-tailed with depth \citep{noci2021precise, zavatone2021exact}, following sub-Weibull distributions \citep{vladimirova2019understanding}. We see this directly, if modestly at this width: both skewness and excess kurtosis grow with depth (\Cref{fig:heavy-tails}). These heavy tails expose a limitation of the diagonal Gaussian propagation pass. In deeper layers, it overestimates on-neuron means ($+1.5\%$ in late layers, $+1.8\%$ at the final layer), while its Gaussian tail underestimates the clipping probability by roughly a factor of two. The submitted estimator largely eliminates this error: kink neurons are sampled, on neurons are eventually treated linearly, and the analytical pass survives only in classification and the dead-neuron correction. How to propagate heavy-tailed distributions well remains an open question.

For kink neurons, a natural next step is to parametrize their joint distribution more carefully, for instance with multivariate probit models \citep{cragg1971some, chib2001metropolis}. There is a clear signal to exploit here: kink pre-activations are substantially cross-correlated, and the coupling grows with depth (\Cref{fig:kink-correlation}). Near-threshold neurons tend to cross the kink together---a joint event that independent-marginal propagation cannot represent.
